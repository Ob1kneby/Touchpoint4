Science as genealogy

The strongest version of the pro-TMT argument: site picked to avoid known shrines and burials, rent and STEM funding for local students, and the "our ancestors navigated by the stars" framing. Then Kaluna's view: support for astronomy, but with limits on building.

The legal fight

Consulted, never asked

The 2018 ruling: removing old telescopes "offsets" a new one, like swapping out old scoreboards. Justice Wilson's dissent: more damage to a damaged summit is still damage. Then Hitt's consultation vs. consent: Hawaiians were heard at hearing after hearing but could never stop anything.
“The whole notion of ‘consultation’ really comes out of the framework where the Indigenous people concerned are fully subservient to the state.”Carla Fredericks, quoted in YES! Magazine (2019)
Back to the question: This is where the answer to "who decides" has been: the state and the courts.

Where it stands now

The speed of trust

Act 255 moves control from UH to a new authority with protectors, practitioners, and astronomers on one board. It's already changing the engineering: TMT is now looking at building where an old telescope was removed. But federal funding may decide TMT before the sacred-site question is ever answered.
“…we should strive to move at the speed of trust, rather than being driven by the expedience of transaction.”John Komeji, chair of the Mauna Kea Stewardship and Oversight Authority, via Civil Beat (2026)
Back to the question: For the first time, the people the mountain is sacred to may get a real say. Will it count?
